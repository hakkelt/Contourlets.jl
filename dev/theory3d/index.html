Theory — 3-D

This page covers the 3-D transforms. For the 2-D constructions see Theory.

The 3-D transforms are not a dimensional generalisation of the 2-D ones. The quincunx lattice, the shear index remap and the Q2345 ladder all rely on properties of the plane that do not survive, and the Laplacian pyramid's critically sampled directional partner has no 3-D analogue at all: Lu and Do prove that hourglass-shaped frequency partitioning cannot be achieved by a critically sampled filter bank above two dimensions. So the 3-D directional stage is a different construction, with a redundancy of $N$ in $N$ dimensions that is unavoidable rather than a design choice.

Nonsubsampled pyramid (NSP3D)

The multiscale stage is the direct extension of the 2-D à trous pyramid. At level $j$ the filters are upsampled by $2^{j-1}$ along each axis and nothing is decimated:

coarse   = h_j ⊛ volume
bandpass = volume − g_j ⊛ coarse

The synthesis rescale _NSP_SYNTH_SCALE is applied per axis, so the 2-D constant carries over unchanged. This is worth stating explicitly because the obvious guess is wrong in an instructive way: the biorthogonal synthesis $g$ has DC gain 2 per axis, giving $2^3 = 8$ across a separable 3-D filtering, and one might expect the correction to become $1/8$. It does not — the $1/2$ applies once per axis, and $(1/2)^3$ is exactly the $1/8$ needed. A wrong normalisation here does not break perfect reconstruction, it merely fills the "bandpass" with low-pass leakage, which is why the test suite checks that a constant volume leaves the bandpass at zero.

All filtering is periodic, so the stage is exactly equivariant under circular shifts and — since periodic separable filtering is circular convolution — an FFT evaluation of it is exact rather than approximate. See conv3d_sep_fft!.

Hourglass filter bank

The directional stage starts by splitting the spectrum into three hourglass-shaped subbands, each aligned with one frequency axis:

\[P_i = \{\omega : |\omega_i| \ge |\omega_j| \ \text{for all } j \ne i\}\]

so subband $i$ keeps the frequencies closer to axis $i$ than to any other.

The design follows Lu and Do's mapping-based construction. One-dimensional polynomials $f_0, f_1$ are composed with a multidimensional mapping kernel $M(\omega)$ — a zero-phase FIR filter designed so that $M \approx +1$ on the desired passband and $\approx -1$ outside it — giving multidimensional filters $f_0(M(\omega))$ and $f_1(M(\omega))$ that inherit both the FIR property and the perfect-reconstruction relation between $f_0$ and $f_1$.

The kernels come from multivariate Bernstein polynomials under the change of variables $x_i = (1 - \cos\omega_i)/2$, which maps $|\omega_i|$ monotonically onto $[0, 1]$. That is the reason the construction is tractable: an hourglass passband is a statement about the ordering of frequency magnitudes, and after the change of variables it becomes the same ordering statement about the integer Bernstein sample indices — see mapping_coefficients.

Three subbands are produced by a cascade of two two-channel banks:

spectrum ──┬─ P₁                             (3-D mapping kernel)
           └─ P₂ ∪ P₃ ──┬─ P₂                (2-D kernel on ω₂, ω₃)
                        └─ P₃

The second stage needs only two frequency variables. That is not an optimisation but a theorem: Lu and Do's Proposition 1 shows the optimal passband support for a tree node with index set $J$ involves $|J|$ frequency variables, so in 3-D the second cut is a 2-D design.

Two deviations from the reference construction

Frequency domain rather than FIR. Lu and Do deliberately chose FIR spatial filters because their earlier frequency-domain design needed too much memory for streaming video. That trade-off inverts here. These stages are nonsubsampled with a periodic boundary, so the FFT is already the default backend, and evaluating the mapping kernel directly on the DFT grid is exact — there is no truncation of the 37³ and 61³ FIR filters the paper reports.

A partition of unity, normalised into a Parseval frame. The 1-D polynomials used here satisfy $f_0(x) + f_1(x) = 1$ identically, so the three weights $W_k$ sum to 1 at every frequency, for every choice of order and poly. What the filters do with that partition is the tight setting of make_hourglass3d:

  • tight = false — the masks are the weights themselves. The synthesis filters are all 1 and reconstruction is a plain sum, with no transforms at all. But $\sum_k W_k^2$ ranges over $[1/3, 1]$, so the bank is a frame with bound ratio 3: analysis does not preserve energy, and white noise arrives with subband-dependent variance.

  • tight = true (the default) — the masks are $\sqrt{W_k}$, the pointwise normalisation SurfBox uses. Then $\sum_k (\sqrt{W_k})^2 = \sum_k W_k = 1$ identically: a Parseval frame, $\sum_k \|s_k\|^2 = \|x\|^2$, with the analysis adjoint as its own inverse. Synthesis reapplies the masks, so it costs a forward transform per subband plus one inverse rather than a bare sum.

The price is smaller than the extra transforms suggest, because the pyramid dominates a 3-D NSCT. At 128³ Float32 with J = 2, L = [2, 2] and 8 FFTW threads: the directional inverse costs 0.294 s tight against 0.096 s loose (3.1×), but the full round trip only moves from 0.66 s to 0.76 s (1.15×).

Both are exact. SurfBox normalises to $\sum_k V_k^2 = N$ rather than 1 — the same construction up to the constant $\sqrt{N}$ — and test/items/test_surfbox_reference.jl checks both against it. Whichever setting is in use, the deviation from Lu and Do's optimised lifting/ladder coefficients costs nothing in frame conditioning; the pointwise normalisation reaches tightness on its own.

The normalisation exposes a constraint the plain-sum path hides: each mask must be even in frequency, or the masked spectrum is not Hermitian and the real subband is a lossy record of it. A plain sum never notices, since the individual asymmetries cancel before the inverse transform. See "A sign trap on the DFT grid" below — the same trap, in the same place, for the same reason.

order (the Bernstein order $N$) controls how sharply the subbands separate. Larger $N$ converges to the ideal indicator; the deviation from it falls monotonically, from about 0.095 mean absolute error at $N = 4$ to 0.024 at $N = 20$.

Directional filter bank (NSDFB3D)

Each hourglass is then refined into wedges. Inside $P_i$ the two transverse ratios

\[s = \omega_a / \omega_i \in [-1, 1], \qquad t = \omega_b / \omega_i \in [-1, 1]\]

parameterise direction, and $l$ levels of refinement cut each into $2^l$ equal slabs. With $l_a + l_b = L$ this gives $2^L$ wedges per hourglass and $3 \cdot 2^L$ directional subbands in total, whose ideal passbands are rectangular-based pyramids radiating from the origin.

Both ratios are invariant under $\omega \to -\omega$, so the masks stay symmetric and a real volume keeps real subbands. Each ratio involves only two frequency axes, so a wedge mask is a matrix: the whole bank stores $2(2^{l_a} + 2^{l_b})$ planes no matter how large the volume is.

Lu and Do build these wedges from a chain of iteratively resampled checkerboard filter banks, because their transform is critically sampled inside each hourglass and the resampling is what keeps it so. This bank is nonsubsampled, so there is nothing to resample for and the wedge boundaries are placed directly in the frequency domain. The ideal partition is the same one. Constructing it directly also avoids the failure mode that a separable approximation invites: filtering the transverse directions with 1-D filters produces stripe and block tilings of the frequency plane, not wedges, and the resulting transform is still perfectly reconstructing — so perfect reconstruction alone does not detect the error. The test suite therefore checks the wedge index a plane wave lands in, not merely that some subband is large.

3-D NSCT

Composing the two stages gives the transform:

volume ─ NSP3D ─┬─ coarse ─→ next scale
               └─ bandpass ─ NSDFB3D ─→ 3·2^L directional subbands

Every coefficient array keeps the size of the input, so the transform is exactly shift-invariant — and correspondingly redundant: $1 + \sum_j 3 \cdot 2^{L_j}$ copies of the input. For $J = 3$ and $L = [2, 2, 3]$ that is 49 volumes, 3.1 GB at 256³ Float32. The streaming form of nsct3d_forward exists for this reason: it holds one subband at a time.

Because the directional bank depends only on the volume size and never on the scale, a workspace plans the hourglass mapping response once and shares it across every scale, with only the (matrix-sized) wedge masks differing.

Running on a GPU

The 3-D stack needs no device kernels. Both transforms are FFT plus a pointwise mask, and every mask is stored as broadcastable factors — the mapping responses and wedge planes are 3-D arrays with singleton axes where they do not depend on a frequency — so applying a mask, splitting a complex volume into real and imaginary halves, and the pyramid's frequency-domain multiply are all plain broadcasts. Moving a plan to the device is therefore just moving its arrays and replanning the transforms:

using CUDA, Contourlets
using CUDA: CuArray
import Adapt

params = ContourletParams3D(J = 2, L_array = [2, 2])
ws = Adapt.adapt(CuArray, make_nsct3d_workspace(params, (128, 128, 128), Float32))
c  = nsct3d_forward(CuArray(vol), params; workspace = ws)

A workspace adapt moves the shared hourglass response once, not once per scale. Verified against the CPU path on CUDA and OpenCL. The decimated surfacelet transform is CPU-only for now: its support-box extraction is an index gather, not a broadcast.

Surfacelet transform (decimated)

The NSCT3D buys exact shift invariance with redundancy. The surfacelet transform trades the invariance back for a smaller coefficient set, which is what makes it usable as a sparsifying transform rather than only as an analysis tool.

The target frequency partitioning is Lu and Do's: pairs of concentric cubes radiating from the origin, at different scales and directions. Scale $j$ covers the shell $\|\omega\|_\infty \in [\pi/2^j, \pi/2^{j-1}]$.

Decimation is done by frequency-domain support extraction. Every mask here has compact support in frequency, so the masked spectrum is restricted to the smallest symmetric box containing it and inverse-transformed at that reduced size — the mechanism the wrapping-based discrete curvelet transform uses. Two consequences:

  • The directional masks are not the Bernstein mapping kernels used by the nonsubsampled bank. Those are the right choice there and the wrong one here: a mapping kernel is a polynomial in $\cos\omega$, so $f_0(M(\omega))$ is nonzero almost everywhere and its bounding box is the whole grid. The requirement inverts, so the construction does: masks are built from soft steps that are exactly 0 and 1 outside a transition band.

  • Perfect reconstruction comes from $\sum V_i^2 = 1$ (the masks are the square roots of a partition of unity) rather than $\sum W_i = 1$. Synthesis is then the analysis adjoint, and the frame is tight in the weighted sense $\sum_i N_i \|s_i\|^2 = N \|x\|^2$, where $N_i$ is the subband's element count.

A sign trap on the DFT grid

On the DFT grid, $\omega \to -\omega$ is not a sign flip of the folded frequency: at the Nyquist bin of an even-length axis $\pi \equiv -\pi$ maps to itself. A sign-sensitive quantity like the wedge ratio $\omega_2/\omega_1$ therefore comes out with opposite signs at two bins that are each other's negation. That makes the masked spectrum non-Hermitian, the subband complex, and — because subbands are stored real — destroys perfect reconstruction, with no error message. The masks are evaluated at a canonical representative of each $\pm$ pair to remove the ambiguity.

The same trap bit nsdfb3d, and stayed invisible for longer. With the masks applied once and reconstruction a plain sum, the individual asymmetries cancel before any inverse transform: $\sum_k W_k X = X$ whatever each $W_k$ does at Nyquist, so perfect reconstruction held while the masks were not even. Turning on tight makes synthesis reapply the masks, which requires each subband to survive an irfft/rfft round trip, which requires the mask to be even on the two self-conjugate planes of the rfft grid. It was not, and reconstruction failed by 0.27.

_wedge_ratio fixes the sign from the first coordinate that can carry one: $\omega_\mathrm{ref}$ when it is neither 0 nor $\pi$, and $\omega_\mathrm{tr}$ otherwise. Each factor canonicalises its own plane independently, so the masks stay separable — the property the GPU path depends on — and nsdfb3d — masks are even in frequency pins the result.

How much it actually saves

Measured redundancy against the nonsubsampled transform at the same parameters:

J, Lsurfacelet3dNSCT3D
1, [1]6.27
2, [1, 1]7.713
2, [2, 2]15.325
3, [2, 2, 3]16.049

So this is a partially decimated transform — about 3× leaner at useful settings, not the redundancy of roughly 4 that Lu and Do report. The reason is worth knowing before trying to tune it away: an axis-aligned box recovers the radial decimation in full (a coarser shell shrinks by 2 per axis) but almost none of the directional decimation, because the bounding box of a pyramid radiating from the origin is nearly as large as the pyramid's enclosing cube. Closing that gap needs a box aligned with the wedge — sheared wrapping, or equivalently Lu and Do's resampled checkerboard chain — and the shear has to be an integer index remap on the sampling grid, which at dyadic wedge boundaries it is not unless the decimation is interleaved with the refinement the way the reference construction does it. That is a missing piece, not a tuning problem.

That analysis is confirmed by the reference implementation. Running SurfBox's Surfdec on a 96³ volume at Pyr_mode = 1.5 gives a redundancy of 3.9259 for 24 directional subbands and the same 3.9259 for 96 — flat in the direction count, as sheared boxes predict. This package's surfacelet3d gives 15.4 and 46.4 at the matching settings, growing roughly linearly. So the gap is not a constant factor; it widens exactly where a directional transform earns its keep.

One further limit worth stating plainly: the reference multiscale pyramid resamples by the non-integer factor 3/2 per axis, which this package's cube-shell octave partition does not do.

What has been checked against SurfBox

SurfBox is available again (MATLAB File Exchange submission 14485), so the directional stages are now regression-tested against it in test/items/test_surfbox_reference.jl, with test/reference/ holding the golden data and the MATLAB scripts that regenerate it. What the comparison establishes:

  • The tight normalisation matches. SurfBox's masks satisfy $\sum_k V_k^2 = 3$ to machine precision; this package's satisfy $\sum_k V_k^2 = 1$, the same construction scaled by $\sqrt{3}$.
  • The hourglass geometry agrees. Over a 32³ grid the two constructions pick the same dominant hourglass at 94.6% of frequencies, with a mean absolute weight difference of 0.037; every disagreement is a near-tie in the reference (median gap 0.005 between the top two weights, against 0.887 averaged over all frequencies), so the two differ only in how they round the transition bands.
  • The wedge partition agrees. Across 80 plane waves at 12 directional subbands, the confusion matrix between SurfBox's subband labels and this package's is a bijection, 84% of directions match exactly under it, and for every one of the 80 the reference's subband is this package's first or second choice by energy. This is the check that matters, because perfect reconstruction alone would pass for any structurally-PR bank, including one with the wrong tiling.

What it does not establish is coefficient-level agreement: SurfBox's hourglass raises the magnitude response of a Kaiser-windowed diamond kernel to a power, this package composes Bernstein mapping polynomials, and its NDFB is a decimated resampled-checkerboard chain against directly-placed frequency boundaries here. Same ideal partition, different discrete realisation.
